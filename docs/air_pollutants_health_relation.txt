1. **PM2.5** (fine particles)
   The heavyweight villain: strongest and broadest evidence for **premature mortality + cardiovascular/respiratory disease**, and it’s pervasive. In Europe, PM2.5 is consistently identified as causing the **largest health impacts**. ([European Environment Agency][1])

2. **NO2**
   A major **traffic-related** pollutant tied to airway inflammation, asthma outcomes, and broader cardiopulmonary risk. Also a useful “this area is traffic-polluted” indicator, though separating NO2’s *independent* effect from the rest of traffic pollution is tricky. ([US EPA][2])

3. **O3** (ozone)
   A classic “bad-air day” gas: strong links to **asthma attacks, hospital admissions, and short-term mortality markers**, especially in warm/sunny periods. ([Environmental Protection Agency][3])

4. **PM10** (coarse particles)
   Still harmful (especially for respiratory irritation), but generally **less potent per µg/m³ than PM2.5**, and the evidence base is typically less strong than for PM2.5. ([GHD][4])

5. **SO2**
   Mostly a **short-term irritant** problem: spikes can trigger **bronchoconstriction and asthma symptoms**, especially near specific sources (industry, shipping). Important when it peaks, but in many cities it’s not the main chronic burden driver. ([Environmental Protection Agency][5])

6. **CO**
   CO is *absolutely* dangerous (it’s an asphyxiant), but **outdoor urban ambient levels** are usually controlled well enough that it tends to be a smaller *day-to-day citywide* health burden than the others; it becomes “top-tier” mainly in **enclosed/indoor or micro-environment** scenarios (garages, faulty heaters, etc.). ([Environmental Protection Agency][6])
